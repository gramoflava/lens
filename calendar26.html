lens · Design proposal · October 2026

One calendar for humankind

Three designs. The Solstice Calendar is the ideal on paper: one grid for every month, forever. Hearth is the calendar for people. Lightcone Time is for travellers near and beyond the speed of light. Below them: 12,000 years of history on one timeline, 17 calendars to explore, and a converter for any date.

Where the idea comes from. The Kurzgesagt video “We Finally Know More About Humanity’s First Civilization” starts history when the ice age ended, about 12,000 years ago, when people began to farm and settle. It proposes to make that time year one of a Human Era calendar: today is the year 12,026. This page starts from that idea and asks the next question: if we change the year count, what is the best calendar to put under it?

Kurzgesagt also publishes a printed Human Era Calendar every year, with the year written as 12,0xx HE. The year count itself is the Holocene Era of geologist Cesare Emiliani (1993): add 10,000 to the Gregorian year.

1 · ideal

Solstice Calendar: the same grid every month, forever

A clean-slate design. It ignores tradition and politics and asks what is easiest to compute, plan and share across the whole planet. Every month has 30 days. A week has 6 days. The date tells you the weekday. Five days a year (six in a leap year) belong to no month and no week. They are holidays for everyone.

Shaded columns D5 and D6 are rest days, in every month of every year. The 17th is always a rest day.

rules

Five rules

Months12 × 30 days

Every month is 5 weeks of 6 days. A quarter is 90 days and exactly 15 weeks. One grid is the calendar for all months of all years.

Week4 work + 2 rest

Days are numbered D1 to D6. D5 and D6 are the weekend. Weekday = (day − 1) mod 6 + 1. Rest is 1/3 of the week instead of 2/7.

Extra days4 Turning + 1 World

One Turning Day closes each quarter, near a solstice or an equinox. World Day ends the year. Leap Day follows it. None of these is in a week.

New yearDecember solstice

Turning Day IV is on the solstice ±2 days. It is the shortest day in the north and the longest in the south: a turning point for both hemispheres.

Leap rule÷4, except ÷128

Mean year 365.2421875 days, against the mean tropical year 365.24219. Gregorian is 365.2425. The rule is from astronomer J. H. von Mädler (1864).

YearsHuman Era

Gregorian year + 10,000, as in the Kurzgesagt video. All of recorded history has positive years. The count belongs to no religion and no state.

2 · human

Hearth: a calendar built for people, not for spreadsheets

A perpetual calendar is ideal for machines. People want something else: familiar names, a week that never breaks, and variety. With a perpetual calendar your birthday is on the same weekday all your life. With Hearth it moves one day each year, so it falls on a weekend about 3 years in 7. Hearth changes the Gregorian calendar as little as possible and fixes only what hurts.

Week7 days, never broken

No day ever leaves the week. Religious weeks and the civil week stay in step. Birthdays and holidays move through the weekdays.

Work4 days + 3 rest

Monday to Thursday. Large trials of the 4-day week (UK 2022, 61 companies) reported less burnout and stable output. Most kept it.

Months30 · 30 · 31

Each quarter is 91 days = 13 weeks. The 31st is always the last day of a quarter. No knuckle rhymes. Names stay the same.

Extra daysYear End + Midyear

Year End Day after 31 December, Midyear Day after 31 June in leap years. Both are holidays, and both keep their weekday.

Fresh startsMany landmarks

People start new habits after “temporal landmarks”: a new week, month, year or birthday (Dai, Milkman & Riis, 2014). Hearth keeps all of them.

ClockNo clock changes

Not a calendar rule, but the same aim: permanent standard time. Sleep scientists link the spring clock change to sleep loss and more accidents.

3 · interstellar

Lightcone Time: date-keeping when “now” is not the same everywhere

A calendar assumes that everyone shares one “today”. Relativity says they do not. Two events that are simultaneous for one observer are not simultaneous for another who moves. A traveller at high speed ages less than the people at home. No calendar can fix this. A good system keeps three clocks and never mixes them.

Clock 1 · where-and-whenReference time

SI seconds since J2000.0 (1 Jan 12000 HE, 12:00 TT). In the Solar System: Barycentric Coordinate Time (TCB). Between stars: the frame at rest relative to the cosmic microwave background. It is the only “preferred” frame nature gives us.

The Sun moves at about 370 km/s in that frame, so Sun time and CMB time drift apart by about 24 s per year.
Clock 2 · your lifeProper time

Each person, ship and AI counts its own lived seconds. Show it in metric units: 1 ks = 17 min, 1 Ms = 11.6 days, 1 Gs = 31.7 years. Age, contracts and medicine use this clock.

Vernor Vinge’s traders in “A Deepness in the Sky” count in kiloseconds for the same reason.
Clock 3 · daily lifeLocal calendar

Each world keeps its own days and years from its own sun: Mars has sols of 24 h 40 min. Earth and ships with Earth culture use the Solstice Calendar or Hearth. Holidays are local.

Order by cause, not by date

Far apart, the order of two events can depend on the observer. Only cause and effect has the same order for all: B can be caused by A only if light from A reaches B in time. So every message carries its reference time, its sender's proper time and position, and a vector clock: the count of events it already knows from each sender. Computers already order events this way. Leslie Lamport's 1978 paper on logical clocks took the idea from special relativity.

If faster-than-light travel exists

Relativity plus FTL gives time travel: a signal faster than light, sent from a moving ship, can arrive before it was sent (the “tachyonic antitelephone”). The only safe rule is to make one frame special. Every FTL jump must be instantaneous in the CMB frame, and must never arrive at an earlier reference time than it left. Then the reference time becomes a true universal clock, cause stays before effect, and the three-clock system still works.

The days that never happened

When a country moved from the Julian to the Gregorian calendar, it removed 10 to 13 days. In Britain, 3–13 September 1752 do not exist. Sweden tried a slow switch, failed, and needed a 30 February.

Sources and method
  • All dates are converted through the Julian Day Number. Algorithms follow Reingold & Dershowitz, Calendrical Calculations (Gregorian, Julian, Hebrew, tabular Islamic, Coptic, Ethiopian, Indian national) and Borkowski / jalaali-js (Persian).
  • Islamic: the Umm al-Qura table of Saudi Arabia for 1343–1500 AH (1924–2077), the tabular rule outside it.
  • Chinese: month data for 1645–2200 computed with the sxtwl astronomical library and cross-checked with two other libraries; where they disagree (6 months in 200 years), the majority is used.
  • French Republican: the equinox rule of 1793 (Paris Observatory) for years I–MCC, computed with PyEphem; Romme's rule outside. Day names: the 1793 calendar of Fabre d'Églantine.
  • Maya: GMT correlation 584283. Solstices and equinoxes: Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, ch. 27 (mean terms, accurate to about an hour).
  • Relativity: constant speed τ = t/γ; constant 1 g with mid-way flip τ = (2c/a)·arcosh(1 + ad/2c²).
Number check
  • Each calendar was tested on 4,000 random dates against independent libraries (convertdate, hijridate, jdatetime, sxtwl): 0 mismatches for Gregorian, Julian, Hebrew, Islamic, Chinese, Maya, Coptic, Ethiopian, Indian national and ISO weeks. Every calendar converts back to the same day.
  • Persian: 698 of 701 new years (1100–1800 AP) match the astronomical rule (equinox before noon in Tehran). The 3 others are within minutes of noon.
  • Ideal calendar: from 12026 to 12890 HE, Turning Day IV is never more than 2 days from the December solstice, and the other Turning Days stay within 3 days of their equinox or solstice.
